# Sports Nutrition Just Admitted Sodium Doesn't Matter on Its Own — The Ratio to Fluid Does

**TL;DR**
- A 2025 review in *Performance Nutrition* (McCubbin) concludes there's no evidence sodium replacement improves performance independent of fluid replacement — it's the sodium-to-water ratio that drives outcomes, not sodium intake in isolation.
- Sweat sodium concentration varies up to ~10x between athletes (roughly 200–2,300 mg/L, average ~950 mg/L) and is largely fixed by genetics (the CFTR gene), which is why a single sweat test is considered good for years, not something to track continuously.
- That genetic-variability finding is the entire premise behind the current wave of sweat-patch products — Gatorade's Gx Sweat Patch (built with Epicore Biosystems) and Nix Biosensors — all racing to precisely instrument the *loss* side of hydration.
- A March 2026 meta-analysis of 26 trials found carbohydrate-electrolyte supplementation improves time-to-exhaustion and blood glucose response, reinforcing that electrolytes work in combination with other inputs, not as a standalone lever.
- None of these tools measure the other half of the ratio — what an athlete actually drinks in response. That's an open instrumentation gap, not a competitive one.

## The Research

The most consequential document in electrolyte science this year isn't a new sweat-sensor launch — it's a review paper that quietly undercuts the premise a lot of the electrolyte industry is built on. Alan McCubbin's 2025 review in *Performance Nutrition*, "Sodium intake for athletes before, during and after exercise," synthesizes the accumulated trial evidence and lands on an uncomfortable conclusion: no study to date has shown sodium replacement improves performance independent of fluid replacement. Sodium doesn't work as a solo act. What predicts outcomes — thermoregulation, plasma volume, perceived effort — is the *relationship* between how much sodium and how much water an athlete takes in together, not total sodium balance or sodium content on its own. The review also notes there's no good evidence athletes need more dietary sodium day-to-day than non-athletes, since renal and sweat-gland regulation already handles most of it; the interesting territory is specifically the sodium:water ratio during and around exercise sessions.

That finding reframes what the last several years of sweat-testing technology have actually been solving for. Sweat sodium concentration is remarkably variable between individuals — a roughly 10x range across athletes, from about 200 mg/L to over 2,300 mg/L, with 950 mg/L as a rough average — and that variability is driven substantially by genetics, particularly CFTR gene activity (the same gene implicated in cystic fibrosis), rather than fitness level or training load. Because it's genetically anchored, sweat sodium concentration is stable enough in a given adult that a single accurate test can inform hydration strategy for years, with only minor drift from heat acclimation. This is exactly why Gatorade built the Gx Sweat Patch with Epicore Biosystems, and why Nix Biosensors has pushed real-time sweat-rate-and-sodium wearables into endurance and college athletics: they're solving the "how salty is this specific athlete's sweat" half of the equation — the numerator of loss.

A March 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 trials on carbohydrate-electrolyte (CHO-E) supplementation adds a complementary data point: CHO-E significantly improved time-to-exhaustion and blood glucose response versus control. But because carbohydrate and electrolytes are bundled in nearly every commercial product tested, the meta-analysis can't cleanly isolate sodium's independent contribution — which is consistent with McCubbin's point that sodium's effect shows up in combination with other inputs (fluid, carbohydrate), not alone.

Put together, the state of the science says: sodium adequacy is a ratio problem, sweat testing has gotten very good at measuring one side of that ratio (loss), and the industry currently has no comparably precise way to measure the other side (actual replacement) outside of self-report or app-based estimation.

## Why This Matters for syp

Every well-funded player in personalized hydration right now — Gatorade/Epicore, Nix, the sweat-testing services marketed to endurance athletes — is instrumenting the numerator of the exact ratio McCubbin's review says actually matters, and leaving the denominator to guesswork. syp is the only piece of that equation that measures fluid replacement directly rather than inferring or self-reporting it, which means it's not a competitor to sweat-patch technology — it's the missing half. That's a sharper fundraising and partnership narrative than "we're a hydration wearable": syp can credibly pitch itself as the intake instrument that makes an athlete's sweat-sodium data actionable, and a natural integration partner for any sweat-testing product that currently ends its value chain at a recommendation the athlete has no way to verify they followed.
