# The Hydration Market Is Splitting in Two — And Only One Half Is Defensible

**TL;DR**
- Epicore Biosystems (Northwestern spinout, sweat-sensing biowearable maker) launched a
  "Hydration Readiness Score" in June 2026 — a reusable armband, companion app, and
  dashboard that turns raw sweat/physiology data into a daily score, explicitly built for
  coaches, health-and-safety leaders, and caregivers monitoring *other people's* hydration,
  not just individual users.
- That launch follows a $26M Series B (expanded to $32M) earmarked to push Epicore's
  biosensing platform beyond hydration into kidney health, women's health, malnutrition, and
  toxin-exposure biomarkers — the company is repositioning from "hydration sensor" to
  general-purpose biomarker infrastructure.
- Forbes (July 2026) documents a split in the broader category: DTC electrolyte/powder
  brands (Waterdrop, Cirkul, air up) keep raising hundreds of millions even as Liquid I.V. —
  the reigning powdered-hydration leader, owned by Unilever — saw growth stall through 2025
  before a 2026 rebound driven by new product lines rather than brand pull alone. The
  article's thesis: a sachet of sodium and potassium is nearly unpatentable, so smart capital
  is rotating toward harder-to-copy hardware and biosensor plays.
- WHOOP's answer to hydration in 2026 is conversational: its AI assistant will talk to you
  about hydration in the context of jet lag and sleep schedule adjustments — advice inferred
  from sleep and travel signals, not from anything WHOOP actually measures about fluid
  intake.
- None of the three lanes — commodity consumables, sweat biosensing, or AI-wrapped legacy
  wearables — measures what actually enters the body at the point of consumption.

## The Research

The most concrete news this cycle is Epicore Biosystems' June 2026 launch of what it's
calling the first "Hydration Readiness Score." The mechanics matter here: this isn't a new
sensor, it's a reframing of an existing one. Epicore's skin-worn patches already measured
sweat biochemistry — electrolyte loss, sweat rate — for a fairly narrow athlete/military
niche. The June release wraps that same sensing layer in a reusable armband, a new
companion app, an expanded dashboard, and, critically, a scoring layer aimed at *the person
responsible for someone else's hydration* — trainers, health-and-safety leaders, caregivers
— rather than the wearer alone. Epicore's own language calls this an evolution from a single
product into a "continuous behavioral and operational intelligence platform." That's a
population-monitoring pitch, not a fitness-tracker pitch.

It's not a random pivot. The June launch is downstream of a $26M Series B closed in early
2025 (later expanded to $32M), led by the Steele Foundation for Hope, which the company said
explicitly would fund expansion beyond hydration into kidney health, women's health,
malnutrition, and environmental toxin exposure — plus deployment through Fortune 500
licensees, the Department of Defense, and NIH-backed programs. Read next to the June
product, the sequence is coherent: raise to build a multi-biomarker platform, then ship the
first "readiness score" product that turns raw physiological data into an actionable number
for an institutional buyer. Nix Biosensors, Epicore's closest sweat-sensing competitor, is
playing a much smaller game by comparison — under $7M in total funding — which is itself a
signal about where capital currently thinks the sweat-biosensing opportunity actually is:
institutional platforms, not individual gadgets.

Zoom out to the consumables side of the category and the picture inverts. Forbes reported in
July that DTC hydration brands — Waterdrop, Cirkul, air up, and others — keep pulling in
large rounds, riding a $7.1B (2026) electrolyte powder market growing at roughly 8.9%
annually, per Grand View Research. But the article's real point is about defensibility:
Liquid I.V., the category's biggest US brand under Unilever, saw growth stall through 2025 —
Nielsen data showed a plateau — before recovering to high-single/double-digit growth in H1
2026, credited to new product lines and expanded usage occasions rather than the core brand's
pull. Forbes' framing is blunt: a sachet of sodium, potassium, and magnesium is close to
unpatentable, so even the category leader can't coast on brand equity alone, and investors
increasingly see biosensor/hardware bets like Epicore's as the harder-to-copy, more
defensible lane.

Meanwhile the big consumer wearables aren't building measurement capability at all. WHOOP's
2026 feature set folds hydration into its AI assistant — a conversational layer that will
discuss hydration when it's relevant to sleep and jet lag adjustment. That's guidance derived
from proxies WHOOP already collects (sleep stages, HRV, travel patterns), not a new
measurement of anything related to water. Oura's 2026 roadmap doesn't feature hydration at
all. The two biggest wearable brands are, at best, talking about hydration secondhand.

Put together: the category is bifurcating into a crowded, low-moat commodity lane
(powders, still capital-flush despite one plateau) and a consolidating, better-funded
infrastructure lane (sweat biosensing, moving from athlete gadget to institutional
readiness platform) — with the incumbent wearables sitting outside both, offering advice
inferred from unrelated signals rather than anything measured.

## Why This Matters for syp

Epicore's pivot — from "sensor" to "readiness score plus a dashboard for whoever manages
someone else's hydration" — is the sharpest signal here, because it validates that the next
layer of value in this category is the score built on top of the data stream, not the raw
data itself. That's directly applicable to syp's roadmap: a mass-change intake stream can
support the same kind of readiness/adherence scoring for a coach, employer, or family
caregiver that Epicore just shipped, without needing sweat chemistry at all. It also sharpens
the fundraising narrative: Forbes' "which hydration bet is actually defensible" framing is
exactly the skepticism investors will bring, and syp's answer — direct mass-based ground
truth of intake, versus Epicore/Nix inferring physiological status from sweat and WHOOP
inferring advice from sleep data — is a genuinely different and harder-to-dismiss position
than "another hydration wearable." Competitively, Epicore's institutional monitoring push
(coaches, safety leaders, caregivers) now overlaps with the same enterprise, eldercare, and
athletic-team channels syp has been eyeing, which makes Epicore worth tracking as a direct
comparable rather than an adjacent technology.
