# Creatine's "It Hydrates You" Story Just Failed Its First Population-Level Test

**TL;DR**
- Creatine is now a mainstream 2026 longevity supplement, sold partly on the claim that it pulls water into muscle cells and improves hydration — a $1.85B market this year, growing fastest among aging adults and women who are new to supplementation.
- The first population-scale test of that claim, a 2025 NHANES analysis of 98,681 adults (Food Science & Nutrition), found the opposite of a clean "more creatine, better hydration" story: both the lowest and highest dietary creatine quantiles are linked to worse plasma osmolality than a moderate middle band.
- Higher creatine intake tracked with *lower* total body water, intracellular fluid, and extracellular fluid volumes — directly contradicting the "creatine hydrates your cells" mechanism marketing leans on.
- The finding was contested enough to trigger a formal letter-to-the-editor and published reply — this is an active methodological fight, not settled science, and the underlying weakness on both sides is that NHANES measures dietary recall, not actual fluid intake or supplement dosing.
- That measurement gap — inferring hydration status from what someone says they ate rather than what they actually drank — is the same gap that shows up everywhere else in hydration research.

## The Research

Creatine had a genuine reinvention in 2026. What used to be a bodybuilding-aisle supplement is now marketed to the longevity crowd on cognitive, bone, and — increasingly — hydration grounds, riding the same wave that put it on "most vital supplement for women" lists this year. The physiological hook is real: phosphocreatine stored in muscle is osmotically active, so it draws water into the intracellular space, a mechanism well established in supplementation trials going back two decades. Wellness content has stretched that into a broader claim — creatine as a hydration enhancer, not just a muscle-performance aid — and the $1.85B 2026 creatine market (11.4% CAGR, per Research and Markets) is expanding fastest exactly where that framing lands hardest: aging adults and women trying supplements for the first time via gummies and chewables rather than gym-culture powder.

That claim had never been tested at population scale until a 2025 analysis by Sergej Ostojic's group, published in *Food Science & Nutrition*, pulled 98,681 adults' dietary creatine data from NHANES cycles spanning 1999–2023, cross-referenced against hydration biomarkers — plasma osmolality in 69,144 people, urine specific gravity, and total/intracellular/extracellular fluid volumes in a 12,369-person subset. The result isn't the linear "more creatine, more hydration" story the marketing implies. It's U-shaped: plasma osmolality was healthiest in a moderate intake band (roughly 4.27–10.19 mg/kg), and both the lowest and highest creatine-intake quantiles showed significantly worse osmolality and higher rates of hypoosmolality (p<0.01) than that middle group. More strikingly, higher intake was independently associated with *reduced* total body water, intracellular fluid, and extracellular fluid — the opposite direction from what the cellular-swelling mechanism would predict if it scaled cleanly from a lab supplementation trial to free-living dietary patterns.

This is contested enough that it produced a real scientific fight: a letter to the editor challenged the study's handling of participants on multiple special diets, and Ostojic's team published a formal reply defending the exclusion criteria as a transparent generalizability-for-interpretability tradeoff. That back-and-forth is worth noting on its own — it means the field itself doesn't yet agree on what this data shows, six months after publication.

The deeper issue neither side can fully resolve: NHANES captures *dietary* creatine from 24-hour recall, not supplement dosing or, critically, actual fluid intake alongside it. A population-level correlation between "how much creatine someone reported eating" and "what their blood looked like at one clinic visit" is a genuinely weak instrument for something as dynamic as hydration status — which fluctuates hour to hour based on what people actually drink, not what they ate two days before a recall interview.

## Why This Matters for syp

This is a live, unresolved research fight sitting on top of exactly the target demographic syp needs — longevity-minded, supplement-literate, blood-panel-buying consumers who are being sold a hydration claim that population data can't actually confirm because nobody in the study measured what these people drank. It's a sharp, timely example for content or investor conversations: even well-funded, large-N population science hits a wall when the underlying exposure (fluid intake) is inferred from proxies (dietary recall, blood draws) instead of measured directly — the same critique syp makes of wearables inferring hydration from HRV, just showing up in an unexpected corner of nutrition science instead. A precision-hydration product is the one thing in this picture that could actually settle whether a given person's creatine routine changes their intake behavior, in real time, rather than waiting for the next contested paper.
