# The Largest Hydration RCT Ever Run Used Smart Bottles, Coaching, and Cash — and Adherence Still Decayed

**TL;DR**
- PUSH (Prevention of Urinary Stones with Hydration), published in *The Lancet* in March 2026, is the biggest interventional hydration trial on record: 1,658 stone-disease patients at six US academic centers, randomized to standard care or a full behavioral package — Bluetooth smart water bottles, personalized fluid prescriptions, reminder texts, health coaching, and financial incentives — with a 2-year follow-up.
- The intervention worked mechanically: urine output rose and stayed measurably higher than control through 24 months. It did not work durably: output peaked around 1.8 L/day at 6 months and had slipped below 1.6 L/day by 24 months — never reaching the 2.5 L/day target the whole program was built around.
- The primary clinical outcome — symptomatic stone recurrence — showed no benefit: 19% in the intervention arm vs. 20% in control (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.77–1.20).
- Pushing fluid intake wasn't free of cost either: more urinary frequency, urgency, and nocturia in the intervention group at 6 and 12 months, and more asymptomatic hyponatremia (12 cases vs. 2).
- The researchers' own conclusion isn't "hydration doesn't matter" — it's that uniform, high fluid targets are hard to sustain even with every behavioral tool in the toolbox, and the field should move toward individualized, context-aware targets instead.

## The Research

PUSH is the kind of trial hydration science has never really had: a multi-site, properly powered RCT that didn't just correlate fluid intake with an outcome, but actually tried to engineer higher intake and then watched what happened next. Run by the NIDDK-funded Urinary Stone Disease Research Network and coordinated by the Duke Clinical Research Institute, it enrolled 1,658 adolescents and adults with a history of stones and low baseline 24-hour urine volume, and randomized them 1:1 to guideline-concordant standard care or a genuinely maximal adherence package — a personalized fluid prescription calibrated to each person's own urine output, a goal of at least 2.5 L/day, Bluetooth smart bottles, reminder texts, coaching, and financial incentives for hitting targets.

The adherence data is the real story, and it cuts both ways. The intervention group's urine output was consistently higher than control's at every checkpoint — 6, 12, 18, and 24 months — so the behavioral package wasn't inert. But the trajectory inside that group tells a less flattering story: output peaked around 1.8 L/day at six months, the point of maximum novelty and engagement, then eroded steadily to under 1.6 L/day by two years. The 2.5 L/day target the entire program was engineered around was never reached, on average, at any checkpoint. This is a textbook adherence-decay curve, and it happened despite stacking every tool the behavioral-science toolkit has: real-time tracking, reminders, human coaching, and cash.

That decay is almost certainly why the primary outcome landed null. Symptomatic stone recurrence — stone passage or a procedural intervention — occurred in 19% of the intervention arm versus 20% of controls (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.77–1.20), a gap with no statistical or clinical meaning. Secondary radiographic outcomes (new stones, growth ≥2mm) and a composite endpoint were likewise unchanged. And the intervention wasn't a free lunch: urinary frequency, urgency, and nocturia were more common in the intervention group at 6 and 12 months, and asymptomatic hyponatremia showed up six times more often (12 cases vs. 2) — not dangerous in this trial, but a reminder that "just drink more" has a physiological floor on the other side too.

Lead investigators Charles Scales and Gregory Tasian were candid about what this does and doesn't prove. Scales: "achieving and maintaining very high fluid intake is more challenging than we often assume." Tasian's framing is the more useful one for anyone building hydration tools: "Rather than asking every patient to meet the same fluid goal, we should determine who benefits from which targets." An accompanying commentary made the same point even more directly — future interventions should frame fluid targets flexibly, aligned to a person's actual work and daily routine, rather than handing everyone the same liter count regardless of whether they're a desk worker or a rideshare driver with no easy bathroom access.

The honest reading isn't that intake-tracking tools don't move behavior — they clearly did, for a while. It's that a fixed universal number, even when faithfully measured and incentivized, is the wrong unit of intervention. The trial's failure is in the target design, not in the instrumentation.

## Why This Matters for syp

This is the most rigorous real-world stress test to date of exactly syp's category — direct, device-measured intake tracking plus behavioral nudging — at a scale (1,658 people, 2 years) no consumer hydration company could run on its own, and it's a useful gut-check rather than a pure endorsement. It validates that measured, real-time intake tracking genuinely changes behavior (output moved, consistently, across every checkpoint) — but it's also hard evidence against shipping a single fixed daily-ounce target as syp's core coaching mechanic, since even clinical-grade incentives and coaching couldn't sustain one for two years. The sharper product and messaging takeaway: lean into syp's ability to set and continuously re-calibrate *personalized, context-aware* targets rather than a static goal, and treat long-horizon adherence — not measurement accuracy, which syp has already solved — as the open problem worth designing against.
