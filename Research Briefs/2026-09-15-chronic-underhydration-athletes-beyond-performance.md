# Sports Nutrition Just Admitted It's Been Measuring the Wrong Hours

**TL;DR**
- A new *Sports Medicine* paper from Rúben Francisco and Lawrence Armstrong (Korey Stringer Institute) argues that decades of athlete hydration research have focused almost entirely on acute, workout-day fluid-electrolyte balance — while ignoring the long-term health cost of chronic, habitual under-drinking in the other 20+ hours of the day.
- The paper's headline number: roughly 58% of athletes are habitual "low drinkers," consuming under 35 mL/kg/day of water — a status that's invisible on standard hydration checks because total body water and blood osmolality can look completely normal while urine stays concentrated and circulating arginine vasopressin (AVP) stays chronically elevated.
- That mechanism isn't new — a 2024 Armstrong/Bergeron/Kavouras paper already linked the same chronic-AVP pattern from habitual low intake to insulin resistance, low-grade inflammation, and long-term cardiovascular/renal risk. What's new is applying it to athletes, a population everyone assumed was hydration-optimized by default.
- Industry is converging on the same insight from the supply side: Gatorade's incoming "Gatorlyte Longer Lasting" (glycerol-boosted, highest-electrolyte formula in 60 years) is explicitly positioned for long travel days and demanding work shifts, not competition — and PepsiCo's own launch messaging cites "150 million Americans" feeling dehydrated, an everyday-life claim, not a sideline-cooler claim.
- Nobody in this conversation — the researchers or Gatorade — has a practical way to tell whether a given person actually crossed the 35 mL/kg/day line on a random Tuesday. That measurement gap is the real story.

## The Research

The Francisco/Armstrong paper, out of the Korey Stringer Institute (the same group behind most U.S. exertional heat illness guidance), makes a pointed argument: fifty-plus years of sports science hydration research has optimized for the wrong time horizon. The field built an entire toolkit — sweat rate testing, sodium loss estimation, in-race fluid protocols — around the two-to-four-hour window of a workout or competition, on the assumption that if an athlete rehydrates well around training, their hydration status is fine. The paper's contribution is showing that assumption doesn't hold: a large share of athletes carry a chronic, low-grade fluid deficit that has nothing to do with what they drink during exercise and everything to do with what they drink — or don't — the rest of the day.

The number driving the argument is stark: about 58% of athletes are habitually classified as "low drinkers," meaning they consume less than 35 mL/kg/day of total water — well under recommended adequate intake. Crucially, this group doesn't show up as dehydrated on the metrics sports medicine normally checks. Total body water and blood osmolality stay in normal range, because the body defends plasma osmolality tightly. What doesn't stay normal is urine concentration and circulating AVP, the hormone that signals the kidneys to conserve water. In chronic low drinkers, AVP stays elevated essentially all the time — a compensatory mechanism that looks like nothing is wrong right up until it isn't.

That AVP mechanism is the load-bearing piece of the argument, and it isn't speculative: a 2024 paper from much of the same author group (Armstrong, Bergeron, Muñoz, Kavouras) had already established that habitually low water intake — affecting an estimated 25-33% of adults in the U.S. and Europe generally — correlates with chronically elevated AVP and its surrogate copeptin, and that this pattern is independently associated with insulin resistance, low-grade systemic inflammation, and elevated long-term cardiovascular and renal disease risk. What the new paper does is apply that same lens to athletes specifically, a population sports medicine has generally treated as a hydration-literate, low-risk group. The 58% figure says otherwise — and it's higher than the general-population low-drinker rate, not lower, which is the counterintuitive part worth sitting with.

The industry side of this story arrived within days, coincidentally or not. Gatorade's newly announced Gatorlyte Longer Lasting — its highest-electrolyte ready-to-drink formula in 60 years, boosted with a plant-based glycerol to improve fluid retention — is not being marketed against a competitor's in-race drink. PepsiCo's own launch language frames it around "long travel days, demanding work shifts, or sustained physical activity" and cites a claim that 150 million Americans feel dehydrated day-to-day. That's a company whose entire brand is built on the sideline cooler explicitly repositioning its flagship electrolyte product around chronic, everyday, off-the-field hydration — the exact behavioral gap the new research is describing.

## Why This Matters for syp

Every instrument named in this research — sweat testing, urine concentration checks, plasma osmolality, spot-check copeptin assays — measures a moment, not a pattern, and none of them can tell an athlete or a researcher whether they crossed 35 mL/kg/day yesterday, or on average this month. That's precisely the gap syp's continuous mass-change tracking is built to close: it's the only method in this conversation that produces an actual, longitudinal daily-intake number rather than an inferred snapshot. There's a two-sided pitch here — to consumers, syp is the tool that tells a "low drinker" they're one before it shows up as elevated AVP; to researchers (including groups like Francisco and Armstrong's), syp is instrumentation that could actually test the 58% claim with continuous real-world data instead of self-report diaries. And Gatorade's own pivot toward "everyday, off-the-field hydration" as a growth market is a free signal that the TAM syp is already built for — daily life, not just workouts — is one the category's biggest incumbent is now spending marketing dollars to validate.
