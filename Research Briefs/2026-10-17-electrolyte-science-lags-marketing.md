# The Electrolyte Industry Is a $38B Bet on a Theory the Latest Trial Just Undercut

**TL;DR**
- A 2026 *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* study of 58 ultra-trail finishers found crampers and non-crampers had statistically identical dehydration markers (body mass loss, urine specific gravity) and serum sodium — but crampers had 2.5-3x higher creatine kinase and lactate dehydrogenase, markers of muscle damage.
- A 2026 ISPOR systematic review screened 792 studies on sodium and athletic performance, kept 19, and concluded the evidence base still can't support clear sodium-intake recommendations.
- A 2022 Nutrients review goes further: there's no documented evidence sodium causes exercise-associated cramping at all, and hyponatremia is driven by overdrinking fluid relative to need, not by sodium loss per se.
- Mayo Clinic's Andrew Jagim and Georgia Tech's Michael Sawka, quoted in a January 2026 NutraIngredients piece, say sodium supplementation matters for a narrow slice of use cases (exercise over ~90 minutes, sweat rates above 1.5-2.0 L/h, "salty sweaters," repeated hot-weather training) — and offers "little benefit beyond flavored water" for everyone else.
- The ~$38B global electrolyte category (LMNT, Liquid IV, Nuun, plus Gatorade/Propel reformulations) is growing faster than the science justifying universal use.

## The Research

The clearest new data point comes from a cross-sectional study of the Penyagolosa Trails ultra (106 km, ~5,600 m of climbing), just published in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* (Martinez-Navarro et al., 2026). Of 58 finishers, 9 (16%) developed exercise-associated muscle cramps. The researchers compared crampers and non-crampers across every variable the "dehydration/electrolyte depletion" theory of cramping predicts should differ — and none of them did. Pre- and post-race urine specific gravity: no difference. Body mass change (a standard dehydration proxy): no difference. Serum sodium before or after the race: no difference (140.7 vs. 139.7 mmol/L postrace — clinically indistinguishable). What *did* differ, sharply: creatine kinase at 24 hours postrace (5,166 vs. 1,940 U/L) and at 48 hours (3,194 vs. 894 U/L), plus elevated lactate dehydrogenase at both checkpoints. Crampers weren't more dehydrated or more sodium-depleted — they had more muscle damage. The sample is small (9 crampers; the authors themselves say 35-37 per group would be the properly powered design), but it lands in the same place as a 2015 161-km ultramarathon study and a 2022 Nutrients systematic review (Veniamakis et al.): "no documented scientific evidence" links sodium to exercise-associated cramps directly. Sodium is "one, but not the only, factor" — and arguably not the main one.

The Nutrients review adds a second, sharper point that the marketing side of this industry tends to invert: hyponatremia — the actually dangerous outcome, as opposed to the merely uncomfortable one — is caused by drinking *too much fluid* relative to sweat and sodium losses, not by sodium loss on its own. Sodium supplementation can blunt the drop in plasma sodium, but only when fluid intake is matched to fluid loss. Oversupply the water and no amount of electrolyte powder fixes it. A freshly surfaced 2026 ISPOR systematic review (792 studies screened, 19 meeting inclusion criteria, spanning 1994-2024) reaches a similarly deflating conclusion at the performance level: some trials show benefits on race time, time-to-exhaustion, and core temperature, but overall "current evidence is insufficient to support clear recommendations on sodium use in athletes." The field's consensus move, voiced by both Jagim (Mayo Clinic Health) and Sawka (Georgia Tech) in a January 2026 NutraIngredients feature, is to stop treating sodium as a universal performance hack and start treating it as a tool for a specific physiological profile: exercise duration past ~90 minutes, sweat rates above 1.5-2.0 L/h, salty sweaters, and repeated hot-weather sessions. Outside that zone, their read is blunt — indiscriminate electrolyte use buys you flavored water.

None of this stops the money. One market estimate puts the global electrolyte category near $38B and growing 6-8% annually, with U.S. powder-sachet volume up 15% in a recent year, propelled by near-ubiquitous podcast sponsorship (LMNT in particular) rather than new trial data. LMNT is also facing multiple class-action suits (Montana, New York, Florida federal courts) alleging its "All Natural" and "Keto-Friendly" claims mask synthetic ingredients — a marketing-overreach story, not a sodium-science one, but a sign the category's claims are starting to draw legal scrutiny alongside scientific skepticism.

## Why This Matters for syp

Every arrow in this research points at the same gap: the actual risk variable — on both the dehydration and the hyponatremia side — is *how much fluid someone has actually taken in relative to what they've lost*, and nobody in the electrolyte category is measuring that. Brands sell sodium as the fix for a problem that the evidence says is really a fluid-accounting problem; syp is the only layer in this stack that produces the number (real-time mL consumed) that would let someone tell, in the moment, whether they're in the "add sodium, it'll help" zone or the "put the electrolyte packet down and just track your water" zone. That's a sharper wedge than "hydration is important" — it's "the industry is guessing at your sodium needs because it can't see your water intake, and we can."
