# Gatorade's Own Data Shows Trained Athletes Are Guessing at Sodium Replacement — and Guessing Wrong by More Than Half

**TL;DR**
- Gatorade Sports Science Institute's first "Body of Science" study measured hydration and fueling in 500+ women across basketball, soccer, and ice hockey — and found they replace only ~20% of sodium lost during activity, against GSSI's own 50% recommendation.
- 43% of participants started their sessions already dehydrated; 57% took in inadequate carbohydrate during exercise, with half consuming none at all.
- Basketball produced the highest sodium losses of any sport measured, and individual sweat-rate variation was wide even within the same sport — undercutting any one-size-fits-all electrolyte dosing guideline.
- Separately, a new ad-libitum rehydration trial (Nutrients, 2026) found that carbonating a drink — even an electrolyte-added one — measurably suppresses how much people voluntarily drink after dehydration, meaning flavor/format changes intake independent of physiological need.
- Both studies land on the same conclusion from different directions: self-directed electrolyte and fluid intake is a poor proxy for what the body actually requires, even among people being paid attention to.

## The Research

Gatorade Sports Science Institute spent 2026 running the first wave of "Body of Science," a multi-year research commitment aimed at closing what the company frames as a real gap: only about 6% of sports science research has historically focused on women. The inaugural study tracked hydration and fueling patterns in more than 500 women across basketball, soccer, and ice hockey — not a lab-simulated protocol, but real practice and competition sessions. The numbers that came back are blunt. Nearly half of participants (43%) showed up to workouts already dehydrated, before a single rep. During activity, they replaced on average only 20% of the sodium they lost through sweat — against GSSI's own stated recommendation of replacing close to 50%. Carbohydrate intake was similarly short: 57% of participants took in inadequate carbs, with exactly half consuming none during exercise at all. Basketball, notably, produced the highest sodium losses of any sport in the study, and sweat rates varied widely even among women in the same sport — the kind of individual variation that makes a flat electrolyte-dosing guideline (a scoop of this, a tablet of that) structurally mismatched to most of the people using it.

What makes this finding sharper than the usual "athletes don't hydrate enough" study is who's reporting it. This isn't a conference abstract from a university lab — it's Gatorade's own sports science arm, with direct commercial interest in electrolyte products, publishing data showing that even among athletes being actively studied (plausibly more hydration-conscious than the general population), actual sodium replacement runs at roughly 40% of the recommended target. That's not a rounding error; it's a structural gap between intention and outcome that no amount of electrolyte-tablet marketing has closed, because the people losing sodium don't have a reliable way to know how much they've lost or replaced in real time.

A second study published this year reinforces the same blind spot from the intake side rather than the loss side. Researchers testing ad-libitum rehydration after mild heat-induced dehydration (about 1% body weight loss) gave subjects plain water, carbonated water, electrolyte-added water, or electrolyte-added carbonated water, all at 10°C, and let them drink freely for three hours. Carbonation alone measurably suppressed voluntary fluid intake compared to plain water — people drank less of the fizzy option even though their bodies needed the same volume to rehydrate. Electrolyte addition didn't fully offset that suppression; it improved fluid retention per unit consumed, but couldn't make up for the lower volume people were willing to drink. In other words, format and flavor are independently steering how much fluid someone takes in, in a direction that has nothing to do with what their body actually needs. Put the two studies together and the pattern is consistent: whether you look at output (sodium lost vs. replaced) or input (fluid needed vs. voluntarily consumed), self-regulated hydration behavior diverges from physiological need in ways people can't self-correct because they can't see the gap.

## Why This Matters for syp

This is about as clean a validation of syp's core thesis as this research beat produces: Gatorade's own data shows that even motivated, monitored athletes under-replace sodium by more than half relative to the company's own guidelines, and a separate controlled trial shows that drink format alone can suppress intake independent of need — both are gaps that are invisible without direct measurement, and neither is solved by better electrolyte formulations or better guidelines alone. That's a stronger pitch for a mass-change intake sensor than for a new electrolyte SKU: syp doesn't need a flavor science team to fix either problem, it needs to make the gap visible in real time, which is the one thing wearables inferring from HRV or skin conductance structurally can't do either. The 6%-of-sports-science-on-women stat is also a live opening — Gatorade is explicitly signaling research investment and market attention toward women's hydration needs, which is a specific, well-funded wedge syp could use in messaging or partnership conversations rather than competing head-on with GSSI's broader athlete base.
